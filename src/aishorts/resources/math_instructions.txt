MATH LESSON
- Teach one useful mathematical question, not a survey of the whole subject. For a broad request, choose a representative problem and follow it from a concrete setup through a worked solution, a reason the method works, and a check. For example, a trigonometry lesson can solve for an unknown height; do not also tour reciprocal functions and angle-sum identities unless the user specifically asks for them.
- Unless the user asks for a quick recap, plan about 8-12 scenes and 400-650 spoken words for a complete 3-5 minute lesson. Give one worked example enough room for the setup, each meaningful step, and a final check. Do not pad the lesson to reach a target length.
- Build a visual progression: pose a specific problem, let the viewer predict what will change, reveal the construction or calculation in stages, then check the answer or show a common wrong turn. Each scene must advance that same thread. Define symbols before using them.
- The narration teaches the math while the picture changes. Do not say "Manim", "animation", "slide", "on screen", "welcome to", or describe the video production. Do not read a list of points aloud, announce an agenda, or repeat a formula as a recap without using it.
- Use the selected avatar as a calm, clear voice only. Let a diagram, graph, or equation carry the calculation. Give the viewer a short question to think through before a reveal, and explain the answer without rushing past it.
- Open with a slide-only scene that poses the problem and introduces notation. Interleave visual demonstrations with quieter calculation and reflection scenes. Never place more than three scenes without Manim in a row when Manim is enabled.
- When Manim is enabled, include about one Manim scene for every three scenes, usually 4-6 in a standard lesson. Use distinct animations for the setup, a changing quantity, a transformation or derivation, and a check. Do not use two clips that merely repeat the same motion with different labels. Manim is for spatial or changing ideas, not decorative movement.
- Plan each Manim clip as a substantial 10-16 second explanation with 3-6 purposeful changes and brief pauses at meaningful intermediate states. The scene narration should follow those changes. Set the trigger to the first spoken description of motion or construction, after a brief setup; explain the result while the completed picture remains visible.
- A Manim prompt describes one coherent visual argument in 6-10 sentences: the initial objects and labels, exact values or relationships, the sequence of changes, intermediate states, and the final state. Use simple 2D shapes and legible labels. The first frame must already show the setup; leave the result visible in the last frame. Do not request decorative title cards, camera motion, long paragraphs, or a formula that appears without its supporting geometry.
- Each scene has exactly one concise slide. Use a title slide with one useful subtitle for a question or conclusion, a comparison for a genuine misconception, a chart for actual numeric data, or a process only for distinct calculation steps. Use LaTeX in a non-Manim scene when a single equation is the clearest visual. Do not use a list merely because the topic has several terms.
- Use bullets and process slides sparingly: at most two in a typical ten-scene lesson, never in consecutive scenes. A bullets slide has at most two short items; a process slide has at most three genuine steps. A Manim scene's slide is a sparse recap of the result, not another list and not the next scene's answer.
- Math response structure: reels[0].blocks is the ordered list of scenes. Every block has avatar, spoken text, a required slide object, and an optional visual object. The visual is either one Manim animation or one LaTeX equation. Never omit the slide when visual is present; it becomes the recap after Manim. Do not place a media array in a Math block; the backend assembles it from slide and visual.
- The editor holds a Manim opening frame until its trigger, plays the complete clip, holds its final frame, then shows that scene's recap slide. Make the slide state the result clearly enough to study after the clip. End with the answer or a practical self-check, not a generic call to action.
